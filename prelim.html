Personal Reflection

What I Learned

Working on the Introduction to Natural Language Processing Concepts activity helped me understand how NLP works with text and human language. Before this, I mostly thought of data as something organized in tables and numbers. The module showed me how unstructured text can be processed using tokenization, TF-IDF, embeddings, and context vectors. I also liked the hands-on part because I was able to use the Chat Playground for summarizing text and the Language Playground for language detection and PII extraction.

One of the most useful things I learned was that NLP is not just about counting words. Different methods represent text in different ways, and each one has its own purpose. TF-IDF focuses more on word importance, while embeddings and context vectors help represent meaning and relationships between words. This made the topic easier for me to understand because I could connect the concepts to how modern systems analyze real text.

Looking back, I think I should have written my IEEE paper a bit longer and explained some parts in more detail. The main ideas were there, but I could have added more examples from the activities and more of my own observations. I also could have expanded on how NLP can be used for things like customer feedback, support tickets, and text classification. Overall, the activity gave me a better understanding of NLP, and it also showed me what I can improve when writing technical reflection papers in the future.

Personal Reflection

What I Learned

Working on the Customer Sentiment & Intent Classifier activity helped me see the difference between writing fixed rules and actually training a model to learn patterns from text. At first, IF/ELSE conditions seem straightforward, but once customer feedback includes misspellings, slang, sarcasm, or different ways of saying the same thing, those rules quickly become difficult to manage. The activity made this clear because the classifier had to recognize different types of customer messages based on patterns in the text instead of depending on a single keyword.

Another thing I noticed was how much the quality and amount of training data affected the model. The early trials with only three examples per class were unstable, with more misclassifications and uneven confidence. Expanding the dataset to ten diverse examples per class, then adding more negative complaint samples, showed me why a neural network needs enough variety to generalize better. I learned that improving a dataset isn't only about adding more examples. The examples also need to represent the different ways people actually communicate.

The feature extraction part also helped connect the activity to the deep learning pipeline. Raw text can't go directly into dense neural network layers, so it first has to be tokenized, normalized, and converted into numerical vectors. Seeing where this happens in the workflow made the process less abstract for me. Overall, the activity helped me understand that building a text classifier depends not only on the model itself, but also on how the text is prepared, how representative the training data is, and how carefully the results are tested when the language is unclear or unexpected.

Personal Reflection

What I Learned

Working on the Customer Sentiment & Intent Classifier: Feedforward Neural Networks vs. No-Code Heuristics activity helped me understand what changes when I move from a no-code tool to building a model directly in Keras. In PT-P1, much of the process happened automatically. This time, I had to think more carefully about tokenization, vectorization, the model architecture, learning rate, and other settings that affected training. Using an Embedding layer, GlobalAveragePooling1D, Dense layers, Dropout, and Softmax made the structure of the neural network feel much more concrete instead of something happening behind a single train button.

The 21 experiments also showed me how easy it is for a model to look successful during training while still performing poorly on validation data. Some of the early runs reached very high training accuracy but stayed below 43% validation accuracy, which made the overfitting obvious. Expanding the dataset first to 195 rows helped, but there was still a gap. After increasing it to 345 rows and adjusting the learning rate and hidden neurons, EXP-18 reached 84.62% validation accuracy. What stayed with me most is that hyperparameter tuning can't make up for missing or weak training data. The loss curves and validation results were what really showed whether the model was learning patterns or simply memorizing examples.

This activity also helped me connect the code to the deep learning concepts discussed in class. I could see where feature extraction happens, how the forward pass produces probabilities, how the loss is calculated, and how the optimizer updates the weights through backpropagation. Overall, PT-P2 made the training process feel less like trial and error because I had a clearer reason for checking each result and changing the next experiment. It gave me a better understanding of the balance between model complexity, representative data, and careful evaluation when building a text classifier.
