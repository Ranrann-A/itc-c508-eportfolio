Personal Reflection

What I Learned

Working on PT-F1 helped me set up the actual retrieval pipeline we needed before running full chunking experiments for LendSuri. Instead of jumping straight into tuning parameters, I had to organize my assigned 126-page Member 2 SEC corpus, build the FAISS vector store with all-MiniLM-L6-v2 embeddings, and connect it to the SEA-LION language model. Preparing the notebook with configurable CHUNK_SIZE and CHUNK_OVERLAP variables also made it much easier to keep later tests consistent.

One of the most helpful parts of this activity was designing the three test questions by difficulty level. The Easy question checked a single record for Fido.ph, the Average question required matching Asteria and Cashbazaar across both the 2021 and 2026 SEC lists, and the Difficult question tested whether the retriever could pull both registry records and a newer impersonation advisory for Moneycat. Seeing how question difficulty depends on how many evidence passages are needed made the testing log much more structured than just asking random prompts.

Overall, PT-F1 showed me that good RAG evaluation starts before the model even generates an answer. Writing clear reference answers, mapping exact PDF page numbers, and separating prompt checks from retrieval checks gave me a solid baseline to build on for the next experiment.

Personal Reflection

What I Learned

Working on PT-F2 showed me how much chunk size and overlap can change what a RAG system actually retrieves, even when the dataset, embedding model, and questions stay the same. Testing 12 configurations from 300-character chunks up to 1,200-character chunks made it clear that smaller chunks are not always better. At 300 characters, increasing the overlap from 0 to 60 raised the chunk count to 574, yet Recall@3 dropped from 0.778 to 0.444 because top-3 retrieval kept pulling multiple fragments from the same Moneycat advisory instead of bringing in the other required records.

Configuration C12 (1,200 characters with 200 overlap) gave the strongest result in this individual test, reaching Precision@3 of 0.667, Recall@3 of 1.000, and MRR@3 of 1.000 using only 194 chunks. It was the only setting that retrieved all three required evidence units for the difficult Moneycat question across pages 111, 28, and 83. At the same time, reviewing the workbook carefully taught me to double-check manual relevance labels, since I noticed a few C10 label mismatches and an inconsistent C11 index-size entry that needed verification.

Overall, PT-F2 made information retrieval metrics like Precision@3, Recall@3, and MRR@3 feel practical rather than abstract. Instead of guessing a chunk size, I could see directly how keeping each SEC record intact inside larger chunks improved evidence coverage for multi-document questions.

Personal Reflection

What I Learned

For WW-F1 & F2, my main responsibilities were setting up the Excel testing log so our group could record every run consistently, coordinating with my groupmates to execute the benchmarks, and researching which Google Colab GPU runtime we should use. Our first attempt on the default free-tier Colab CPU timed out after about 40 minutes without finishing even one answer, which made it clear we needed a dedicated GPU runtime to test an 8-billion-parameter model like Llama-SEA-LION-v3-8B-IT.

After comparing Colab's runtime options, I recommended the NVIDIA L4 because it offered the cheapest compute-unit rate for a GPU capable of handling our workload, especially compared to an A100. Beyond saving compute units, the L4 gave us 24 GB of VRAM (around 22.5 GB usable in Colab, compared to 16 GB on a T4), which gave the 8B model, embeddings, and prompt context enough headroom without memory errors. Its Ada Lovelace Tensor Cores with FP16 and FP8 support are also built for low-latency inference, and in practice it brought our mean response time on C2 down to 10.87 seconds compared to 485.94 seconds on High-RAM CPU, a 44.72x speedup.

Organizing the testing log also taught me to be careful when interpreting group benchmark results. Even though C2 (1,200/200) achieved the highest speedup and 8/9 accuracy on the L4, the 0.02-second difference between C2 and C3 on GPU was too small to treat as a real speed advantage without more trials, and sampling variation could also affect answer quality between runs. Separating the timed hardware benchmarks from the later conversation-aware chat interface kept our findings honest and easy to verify.

Personal Reflection

What I Learned

Building LendSuri for our final project pushed me to connect every part of the RAG pipeline into a working, auditable system. On the technical side, I handled the core C1 ChromaDB and Llama-SEA-LION-v3-8B-IT implementation, maintained the experiment logs and exported vector-store checkpoints, and connected my groupmate's HTML/CSS/JS design to the live Colab backend. When our initial baseline only reached 62.5% correctness and 41.7% groundedness, I upgraded retrieval by combining semantic search with BM25 over reconstructed record pages using Reciprocal Rank Fusion and exact platform-name boosting. That change lifted mean correctness to 87.5%, raised groundedness to 75.0%, and cut hallucination flags from 58.3% down to 25.0%, while still abstaining on all four unanswerable questions.

During development, I also used generative AI tools like ChatGPT to help with planning, troubleshooting Python and Colab errors, drafting and refining code for the hybrid retriever and UI integration, and organizing experiment records. At the same time, I made sure never to treat AI suggestions as actual experimental results. Every claim, retrieved record, and metric had to be checked directly against the 379 source PDF records, real Colab executions, and our reviewed evaluation sheets so that recorded limitations and errors were reported honestly.

Looking at the finished chatbot, the biggest lesson for me was that reliable AI engineering is mostly about discipline and verification. Saving persistent checkpoints, hashing source files, keeping unanswerable test cases in the evaluation set, and testing the live UI end to end made LendSuri feel like a real, reproducible software system rather than a quick notebook demo.

Final Course Reflection

My Reflection on ITC-C508

Looking back across Prelim, Midterm, and Finals, ITC-C508 changed how I think about building AI and deep learning systems. In Prelim, I started with basic NLP concepts, a no-code text classifier, and Keras feedforward neural networks, where I learned that clean, representative training data matters just as much as hyperparameters. In Midterm, implementing a RAG hallucination stress-test and revising our rejected V1 project proposal into LendSuri taught me how important it is to narrow a problem statement and enforce strict prompt and retrieval boundaries before writing code.

By Finals, those foundations came together in a complete end-to-end project. Running 12 chunking configurations, benchmarking High-RAM CPU against the NVIDIA L4 GPU, and upgrading LendSuri to hybrid semantic and BM25 retrieval showed me that real-world performance depends on measuring every change carefully instead of guessing. Leaving the course, I feel much more confident designing, debugging, evaluating, and honestly documenting AI systems from raw datasets all the way to a working user interface.
